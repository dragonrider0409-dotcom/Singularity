The standard

One underwriting
discipline, three
expressions of it.

The strategies look different from the outside. Underneath, every one of them is checked against the same four questions before capital moves.

Cash first

Does it pay for itself?

We underwrite to cash the asset generates now, not to a multiple someone else might pay later. Exit assumptions are a bonus case, never the base case.

After the owner

Does it work without the seller?

Owner earnings get converted to post-management economics before any multiple is applied. If the business is the person, it is not a business we can own.

Survivable debt

What breaks it?

Every capital structure is stressed against a demand shock and a rate move at the same time. If it only clears in the base case, it is priced wrong.

Duration match

Is the money patient enough?

Long assets get long capital. We would rather pass on a good asset than fund it with money that has to leave before the thesis plays out.

The firm

Owners of businesses, not managers of them.

Reimann Vista Partners was built around a single observation: most capital in the lower middle market is priced for a story, financed for a boom, and held for exactly as long as the fund's clock allows. We do the opposite on all three counts.

What we believe

The businesses we buy are rarely exciting. They collect the same revenue this month that they collected last month, from customers who would notice if they stopped calling. That predictability is the asset. It is also the thing most buyers underwrite away by loading the balance sheet until a single soft quarter becomes a covenant conversation.

We are dealmakers and owners. We do not run the businesses we acquire day to day — we install and back people who do that better than we would, and we hold them to numbers agreed in advance. That division of labour is deliberate, and it is the reason we can look at more than one asset class without doing any of them badly.

The three mandates exist because our capital has three different clocks. Money that must be liquid should not be in a building. Money that can wait fifteen years should not sit in a quarterly-redemption fund earning a liquidity discount it does not need. Matching the two is most of the job.

Absolute Return

A hedged book of public credit and equity, run to steady the firm rather than to top a league table.

The strategy exists for two reasons. It gives investors a place to hold capital that is committed to us but not yet called into a private deal, and it gives the firm a source of liquidity when private pricing turns attractive faster than fundraising can move.

Positions are sized so the whole book can be unwound inside a week. We hedge the exposures we are not being paid to hold — broad market beta, duration, currency — and keep the residual concentrated in situations where we can name the specific reason the price is wrong.

  • LiquidityQuarterly, 45 days' notice
  • InstrumentsPublic equity, corporate credit, listed hedges
  • ConcentrationNo single position above 8% of NAV
  • Hard ruleNothing we could not exit in five trading days

Buyouts & Growth Equity

Control positions in profitable lower-middle-market businesses, and structured minority capital for owners who are not ready to leave.

We look for companies doing between one and six million dollars of EBITDA with revenue that repeats: contracted service routes, maintenance agreements, regulated or licence-gated work, anything where the customer's cost of switching is higher than the cost of staying. Growth is welcome but it is not what we pay for.

Before applying any multiple we convert seller's discretionary earnings into what the business actually earns once a market-rate manager is in the chair. That single adjustment is where most lower-middle-market deals quietly go wrong, and we would rather lose an auction than skip it.

Debt is sized against a stressed case, not the base case. Where a seller wants to stay involved we will structure rollover equity, seller notes or an earn-out — but the operating plan does not depend on them staying.

  • Cheque size$2M – $15M equity
  • Target EBITDA$1M – $6M
  • OwnershipMajority preferred; structured minority considered
  • SectorsEssential and route-based services, light industrial, B2B services
  • AvoidProject-based revenue, single-customer concentration, owner-dependent sales

Real Estate

Assets bought for current income and held long enough that the sale date is a decision rather than an obligation.

Every asset has to service its debt from in-place cash flow at today's rates on the day we buy it. If the deal only works on a pro-forma rent roll, a refinancing, or a cap rate lower than the one we paid, it is not a deal — it is a bet on the exit environment.

We fix rate exposure across the hold wherever the market allows it, reserve for the capital events we can already see on the schedule, and hold leverage at the level where a coverage test still clears with a meaningful margin.

The result is a book that will look slow in a hot market. That is the intended outcome.

  • Asset typesMultifamily, small-bay industrial, necessity retail
  • Deal size$5M – $40M total capitalisation
  • Hold7–15 years, extendable
  • LeverageSized to debt service coverage, not maximum proceeds
  • GeographyMountain West and Southeast growth corridors

What happens after close

The first hundred days are the ones that decide the outcome.

Week one

Nothing changes

Customers, pricing and staffing hold steady. The first job is to understand the business as it runs, not as the data room described it.

Weeks two–six

Instrument the business

Weekly cash reporting, a real chart of accounts, and a scoreboard the operating leader owns. Most acquired companies have never had one.

Weeks six–twelve

Fill the seat

A dedicated operating leader is in place and accountable to a plan agreed before close, with the seller transitioning out on a defined schedule.

Ongoing

Capital, not management

The partners decide where cash goes — debt paydown, add-ons, distributions — and stay out of the day-to-day running of the business.
